SPCL-1

Beyond Orbit: Democratising Access and Launch Infrastructure

A rocket built from a champagne glass and a golf bag.

SPCL-1 is a low-cost, home-built sounding rocket, designed entirely from repurposed household materials and verified through flight simulation rather than a live launch. The goal: show that a working rocket design doesn't require an imported kit.

The problem

Rocketry kits don't travel well.

Commercial rocket kits and motors are expensive and difficult to import across Africa, which keeps hands-on space education and real payload testing out of reach for many university and grassroots teams. The barrier is cost and access — not interest, and not talent.

The approach

Every part is something you can already buy at a party store.

Nose cone

A plastic champagne flute

Pre-shaped and near-zero cost. Sanded down to match the fuselage diameter.

Parachute bay

A gas-relief pill bottle

Doubles as the nose-to-body shoulder and houses the recovery parachute.

Fuselage

A golf club divider tube

Rigid, correct diameter for standard motor mounts, and reused rather than bought new.

Open caveat: rod exit velocity (13.6 m/s) sits just under the 15 m/s guideline for reliable fin stabilisation off the rod — consistent across both runs, so it's a real design characteristic, not noise.
Why not WiFi for a real build? WiFi's usable range is tens of metres — a rocket climbing to 150+ m and drifting under a parachute would drop the connection almost immediately after leaving the pad. Real rocket and CanSat telemetry uses LoRa instead: a long-range, low-power radio link that can hold a connection over kilometres. Adding real instrumentation would mean a small LoRa transmitter plus GPS/altimeter in the parachute bay, paired with a ground-station receiver — the same kind of ground-tracking concept explored earlier for this hackathon track, not a WiFi link.
